The Habits Behind the Typeface

My reason for creating this typeface is simple: I am someone who loves making ceramics. The images on the right show some of the pieces I have made by hand. There is something about the sandy, rough texture of clay between my fingers that makes me feel genuinely close to nature. I wanted people to look at this typeface and immediately think of that earthy quality, the quiet pleasure of making something with your hands.

Glaze, too, is a wonderfully unpredictable thing. Its final color is almost impossible to anticipate before it comes out of the kiln.

A Problem Every Ceramics Studio Knows

Because glaze colors shift so dramatically in firing, most studios keep a reference wall: tiles lined up in rows, each labeled with a color name or number, so students and visitors can get a sense of what to expect. But these labels are almost always handwritten, and the result is often chaotic. Color names become hard to read, numbers blur together, and handwriting varies from person to person. I wanted to design a typeface that could serve this system, one with high legibility, while still holding onto the warmth and playfulness of something made by hand.

Kiln Structure Inside the Letters

This design decision came later in the process. At a certain point, I felt the typeface was too generic. It lacked a defining character, and it was difficult to build a visual system that felt immediately recognizable. That is when I turned to the structure of an electric kiln for inspiration. The interior geometry of a kiln, the shelves, the walls, the way space is organized within, offered a formal logic that felt both functional and specific. It gave the letterforms something to borrow from.

A brief note: electric kilns are remarkable for their precision and accessibility. Unlike wood or gas kilns, their interior structure is clean and modular, a quality that translates naturally into type design.

Reference

Finding references was where I spent the most time, and the most frustration. The design had two seemingly opposing requirements: I wanted the irregular warmth you find in a studio's hand-painted signage, but I also wanted the clarity and geometric consistency of a modern type family, something in the spirit of Klim. Most of what I could find skewed digital and cool-toned, because screens tend to reward that kind of crispness. I kept hitting a wall until I stopped trying to make a single image satisfy both directions at once. The solution, in the end, was to keep the vector work clean and structured, then layer ceramic texture in afterward. The roughness is added as a second pass. It lives on top of the forms, rather than being built into every stroke.

The First Sketches

The earliest version of this typeface was drawn by hand. At that stage, the kiln structure had not yet entered the picture, so I felt uncertain about the direction of each letter: what was guiding each decision, and why. The one thing I kept coming back to was this: even if everything else in the studio fades into the background, the label should always be readable. Nothing here is final. But these first sketches are honest. They show that this project began as a response to a practical need, not as a stylistic exercise.

Thickening the Horizontals to Make the Typeface Stand Out

At one point I experimented with bold horizontal strokes, hoping they would give the letterforms more presence and allow the horizontals to connect across letters so I could set them in different colors. It seemed promising on paper. But the moment I brought those forms into Glyphs, something immediately felt wrong. The original intention had completely disappeared, and what remained was neither readable nor functional. I had no choice but to let the idea go.

The Kiln Shape That Had to Leave

This is also when I introduced the kiln shape into the letterforms. The reference felt right conceptually, but in practice the forms became too rigid. Legibility collapsed almost entirely, and legibility is not negotiable for a typeface meant to serve a working studio system. The kiln structure stayed as an influence, not as a literal template.

When the Drawings Finally Agreed, and the Fixes Multiplied

Here the direction finally held. The letters read as one family, and every half-hidden flaw showed up at once: counters, joins, texture seams, the details you only see when you read strings, not single glyphs.

The pipeline set: structure and ceramic texture in Figma, outlines in Illustrator, solids in Glyphs for spacing and proofs. Geometry stayed digital; the fired look lived in the texture pass. That split ended the loop of hunting Pinterest for a warmer sans and never landing one.

Paragraph Tuning

The big decisions were done. What remained were small moments of visual friction: corners redrawn, joins reconsidered, shapes that looked right in isolation but wobbled in a paragraph.

The lowercase e was the last to behave. The fix was simple: open a long block of text and notice which letter keeps catching your eye. If it stands out while you are trying to read, it has not yet learned how to be boring. Here, boring is a compliment.

The Last Passes

The typeface is essentially complete. I am grateful to Professor Sabrina Nacmias and Professor Eric Doctor for their generous feedback throughout the process. I kept revising in response to their notes, and the result is a set of letterforms I am genuinely satisfied with.
