The habits behind the typeface

Before any brief language, there is the table: glaze tests sitting next to lopsided cups, phone snapshots of pieces you still care about, the small mess that proves the studio is real. These images are only from that everyday folder—personal work, not a mood board imported from the internet. They are here so you know the typeface was written from inside the practice, not from outside commentary.

Labels that outlast the glaze test

The practical trigger is boring and universal: you unload a kiln, pick up a test tile, and the note that should identify the glaze is already gone. I wanted a display face that could survive small sizes, bad light, and the honest clumsiness of studio handwriting—something that reads like equipment for the bench instead of decoration for a slide deck.

Kiln structure inside the letters

A deliberate design layer entered here: every character was asked to obey the same kiln logic— horizontal shelves, shared registers, strokes that behave like load-bearing plates. The goal was not literal illustration but a family that stacks and aligns the way kiln furniture does. The photographs in this strip come only from the kiln research folder, because this is the moment the typeface borrowed structure from heat and brick instead of from calligraphy books alone.

Reference

The pin board mixed three specific typefaces with a long Pinterest scroll. I stayed stuck for a long time because the brief pulled in two directions: I wanted the irregular warmth of studio marks, but I also wanted the readable geometry you see in contemporary families like Klim’s. Most of the references I could collect were digital and cool, because screens reward that clarity. I stayed frustrated until I stopped asking a single drawing to do both jobs. The workaround was to keep the vectors disciplined and add a ceramic skin afterward—texture as a second pass, not a fake wobble drawn into every stem.

The first marks on paper

The earliest pass lived on a single phone capture: blunt weight, short strings, no personality polish yet—only the insistence that a label should still read after everything else in the studio has smeared. Nothing here pretends to be final; it is the proof that the project started as a tool request, not a styling exercise.

When the grid started to boss the drawing

The next notebooks tightened everything: bold strokes stayed, but horizontals were forced to share one width and one vertical register. On paper the discipline read as progress; in feeling it quietly erased the looseness I liked. This carousel pulls every image saved in that phase— scans, exports, and letter tiles—so you can see how far the rule traveled before I questioned it.

The kiln-shaped idea that had to go

The kiln metaphor hardened into something literal: midstrokes bridged like shelves, interiors turned into stacked slabs, and running text almost stopped functioning. Almost everything from this pass was discarded, but it clarified what not to do. The carousel runs every drawing kept from the detour—layout studies alongside the rigid letter exports that show exactly how locked-in the structure had become.

When the drawings finally agreed — and the fixes multiplied

This is where the direction stopped wobbling. The letters finally felt like one family, but the backlog of small wrongs exploded—counters, joins, texture seams, everything that only shows up once you stop looking at single glyphs. The production path also locked: draw clean structure in Figma, add the ceramic texture there, move through Illustrator to expand outlines, then paste the solids into Glyphs for spacing and proofing. Geometry stayed digital while the fired surface lived in the texture pass, which is how I escaped the earlier trap of hunting for a “warmer” sans-serif on Pinterest.

Paragraph tuning

By this point the big moves were finished and the cruel part began. Shapes that looked fine alone still wobbled inside paragraphs; odd corners were redrawn so they followed the same logic as their neighbors instead of hiding behind instinct. The carousel cycles through every file saved from that stretch—screens, vectors, and proofs—because the work was mostly repetition with a sharp eye.

The last passes

The overall voice was settled; what remained were small optical fights. The lowercase e refused to disappear inside paragraphs, which is the usual villain and still humbling. The method that finally helped was embarrassingly direct: keep a long paragraph open and notice which letter keeps tapping your shoulder. If it still sparkles in quiet reading, it has not finished learning how to be boring—and boring, here, is a compliment.

What Grog is for

Grog is a display typeface built from studio logic: a clear skeleton, a ceramic surface, and the patience to let letters be corrected by real paragraphs. It ships in two states—raw and glazed—because clay has more than one honest finish.
